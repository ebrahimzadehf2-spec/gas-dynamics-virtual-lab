Chapter 6 corner drawings

Oblique-shock and expansion experiments default to Analytical contours with a muted rainbow palette: a horizontal approach wall, one compression ramp or opening corner, hatched solid surface, streamlines, wave rays and regions 1 and 2. Drawing style can switch to the unfilled Textbook schematic. Pressure, Mach number, temperature, density and speed can be selected for the color field. Corner speed is u/u₁; interaction speed is dimensional m/s. The numerical color scale uses the same muted palette as the field. No vertical leading face or extra fluid below the wall is drawn.

Chapter 6 uses horizontal incident flow along the upstream wall. Ramp and expansion inputs specify the turn from this incoming direction; no independent angle-of-attack control is provided. Legacy saved incidence values are ignored. Arrowheads on the analytical streamlines show the flow direction before and after each wave. Expansion streamlines follow constant mass streamfunction within the centered fan; they are not CFD trajectories. Slip-line and regional angles are measured from the horizontal incoming direction.

Angle of attack

In chapter 9 positive α inclines the incoming velocity upward relative to the body axis, and the schematic draws that angle.

The chapter 9 linearized model already includes α in Cp, lift and wave drag. Its small-disturbance restrictions still apply. A cone at nonzero α has a three-dimensional, non-axisymmetric flow; replacing its cone angle by θ꜀ ± α in a Taylor–Maccoll calculation is not valid. Chapter 10 provides only the zero-incidence axisymmetric Taylor–Maccoll solution. It has no angle-of-attack input or nonzero-incidence preview. No 3D CFD solver is used.

Supersonic Airfoil Lab · Chapters 6 and 9

Geometry entry defaults to segment angles and lengths. Angles are signed directions relative to the chord (positive upward), not successive corner turns. Lengths are actual straight segment lengths divided by chord, Lᵢ/c, not their x projections. Enter the initial segment(s); the last segment on each side is calculated automatically to close at the fixed trailing edge (x/c,y/c) = (1,0). Its angle and length appear in the Auto row. The diamond mirrors the upper surface; the asymmetric double wedge allows one entered segment per side plus closure. Custom panels support up to 19 entered segments per side plus closure, including add/remove controls. The engine converts a segment using Δx/c = (L/c) cos θ and Δy/c = (L/c) sin θ. Entered segments must stay before x/c = 1, use |θ| < 85°, and stay within |y/c| ≤ 0.3. The completed surfaces must not cross. Custom geometry can switch to the advanced Coordinates mode without changing the shape. Previous saved coordinate or dimension inputs are migrated to equivalent segments.

A common geometry supports a flat plate, a symmetric diamond with adjustable peak location, an asymmetric double wedge with a specified peak camber, or custom upper and lower piecewise-straight surfaces. Coordinates use chord c = 1. For custom panels, enter 2–21 x/c,y/c pairs per side, separated by semicolons or newlines; each surface must begin at 0,0 and end at 1,0. Coordinates must increase in x, remain within |y/c| ≤ 0.3, and surfaces cannot cross. The body-fixed x axis runs from leading edge to trailing edge. Positive α inclines the freestream upward relative to the chord; the upper surface expands and the lower surface compresses on a flat plate.

Shock–Expansion marches from the leading edge along each surface independently using weak attached oblique shocks or Prandtl–Meyer expansions at the actual change in flow angle. Each finite shock reports before/after properties. Attached shocks and supersonic surface states are required; unsupported solutions are flagged and omitted. These are uniform panel states. The actual airfoil edges are drawn in black while a solution is pending, then each edge is colored by its accepted panel Cp using a saturated rainbow scale. The interior remains neutral; no colored ribbon or filled exterior field is drawn. Thin solid black lines represent shocks, and blue multi-ray fans represent expansions. Finite local wave rays are schematic, and wave intersections, trailing-edge pressure matching, the wake, detached shocks, viscosity, boundary layers and finite-span effects are not solved. A curved or blunt airfoil cannot be represented as a validated general solution merely by adding vertices.

Linearized theory uses Cp,U = 2(y′U − α)/√(M∞² − 1) and Cp,L = 2(α − y′L)/√(M∞² − 1), with α in radians. Surface perturbation potential is ψ = φ/(U∞c) = −½∫Cp dξ, where ξ = x/c; the perturbation velocity convention is u′ = ∂φ/∂x. Integration constants set ψ = 0 at the leading edge on each side, and ψ is continuous at each panel corner. This is a first-order surface approximation, evaluated on the chord reference line; it is not an exact potential field through finite shocks. Total surface potential is Φ/(U∞c) ≈ ξ + ψ to first order. Upper and lower potentials need not match at the trailing edge for lifting flow; a wake cut can carry the potential jump.

Panel pressure forces are integrated over the closed geometry. Nonlinear forces are resolved exactly along and normal to the incident velocity. The linearized coefficients retain first-order lift and moment and second-order wave drag; they are not simply an exact rotation of approximate pressures. The quarter-chord moment coefficient is positive nose-up, with reference x/c = 0.25. All coefficients use freestream q∞ = ½γp∞M∞² and unit chord. Drag is inviscid pressure/wave drag only. Compare mode uses the same shape and inlet for both models, shows Cp curves together, and reports signed differences SE − linear. Below the illustration, the Aerodynamic coefficients table compares lift, wave drag, quarter-chord moment, lift-to-drag ratio and the pressure coefficient Cp for each surface region. Cp is local to each region, whereas lift, drag and moment are integrated airfoil coefficients. A separate Flow properties by region table reports local surface states. Geometry intervals, panel angles and lengths are omitted from these tables; angle/length inputs and the automatic closing segment remain in the geometry editor. Coefficients are not repeated in the right-hand result list. Colored surface strips show Cp for the shock–expansion model when available, otherwise the selected linearized model; they do not imply a resolved exterior field.

Input Parameters

Use a slider for continuous adjustment or type a precise number into its numerical field. Numerical entry and sliders are synchronized. Units appear beside each parameter; the engine converts displayed values to SI internally: Pa, K, m/s, J/kg and radians. Slider endpoints show the available interface range; consult Mach input ranges below for model limits.

For preset gases, γ and R are supplied automatically and their editing fields are hidden. Select Custom gas to display and edit these properties. Gas properties and heat capacities remain constant during each calculation, including at high Mach numbers. Upper Mach entry limits are computational limits, not physical-validity guarantees.

Working gas

Choose Air, Nitrogen, Oxygen, Hydrogen, Helium, Argon, Carbon dioxide or Custom gas. Presets use rounded low-density ideal-gas properties at 300 K from Ohio University’s ideal-gas property table. Air retains the book convention R = 287 J/(kg·K), γ = 1.4. Heat capacities are computed consistently as cₚ = γR/(γ − 1) and cᵥ = R/(γ − 1); they remain constant when temperature changes. These presets do not establish real-gas validity at a given pressure or temperature. Custom gas allows independent positive R and γ > 1. The shock-tube interface applies the selected gas on both sides. Dimensionless shock, expansion and cone solutions depend on γ; R sets dimensional density and speed. Linearized Cp, lift and drag coefficients in chapter 9 are independent of both γ and R under that approximation.

What the engine solves

All calculations run locally in your browser using native JavaScript and a background worker. The model is a calorically perfect ideal gas. Internally, units are Pa, K, m/s, J/kg and radians. For air with γ = 1.4 and R = 287 J/(kg K), cₚ = 1004.5 J/(kg K).

Results and selectable plots

Move the pointer or tap inside a plot to show the nearest plotted sample beside the pointer. A point marker and vertical guide identify that sample. The tooltip includes the axis quantities and values; for multiple curves it also identifies the selected curve. Coincident x positions on opposite sides of a shock are distinguished using the pointer height.

Numerical results and state tables appear in the right column on desktop. The plot-property menu below the flow illustration selects a quantity with its own units and axis. The isentropic experiment offers all static-to-stagnation and sonic-reference ratios, critical area ratio, flow angles and dimensional properties. Duct curves show theoretical loci anchored to the inlet; not all points are reachable from a prescribed inlet. Region comparisons and signed control-volume contributions use separate points rather than a spatial trajectory. Shock-tube property profiles use the same analytical similarity solution and demonstration time as the illustration.

Cone contour inspection

Move the pointer or tap inside the colored cone shock layer to inspect polar angle, Mach number and static pressure, temperature, density and speed ratios to the freestream. The probe interpolates the integrated radial and polar velocities and recovers thermodynamic properties from the same post-shock isentropic state. It works on both sides of the meridional view. No value is assigned inside the solid cone, beyond the shock or outside the drawn layer. At the apex, polar direction is undefined. Ratios are shown because dimensional freestream temperature and pressure are not inputs in the cone interface.

Conical-shock attachment limits

Two reciprocal limits are reported for the selected gas: the maximum cone half-angle θc,max at the current freestream Mach number, and the minimum Mach number Mcrit admitting an attached conical shock at the current cone half-angle. The angle limit is the maximum of the Taylor–Maccoll cone-angle curve as shock angle varies, where the weak and strong branches meet. Mcrit is found by inverting that limit. Above θc,max, or below Mcrit, the shock must be detached within this ideal sharp-cone model. The limiting state itself is marginal attachment. The cone Mach input extends down to 1.0001, allowing the near-sonic critical Mach of slender cones to be inspected. These are shock attachment limits, not the sonic surface boundary and not viscous boundary-layer separation. Detached conditions keep the threshold outputs visible but do not show an invented flow field. The search uses the chosen γ and caches repeated results; changing only R does not affect the limits. A missing Mcrit means no root was resolved within the documented numerical search up to M = 128.

Cone-to-shock profiles

The Taylor–Maccoll solution integrates the entire shock layer from the conical shock to the cone surface. Select a property below the illustration to plot it in the opposite display order: cone surface on the left, immediately downstream of the shock on the right. Endpoint markers identify both boundaries. Mach number, static pressure, temperature, density, speed, radial and polar velocity components, flow direction and stagnation ratios are available. Cone profiles use the state immediately downstream of the shock (subscript 2) as their reference, matching Figure 10–10: p/p₂, T/T₂, ρ/ρ₂, u/u₂, Vᵣ/Vᵣ₂, Vθ/Vθ₂, p₀/p₀₂ and T₀/T₀₂. Each normalized curve reaches 1 at the shock. Mach number is shown directly as M, without division by its post-shock value. Flow direction remains an angle in degrees. Sound speed is omitted from the cone plot menu. The contour scale and contour inspection labels retain their explicitly marked freestream reference. The interval contains the smooth post-shock field; the upstream shock jump is not connected as a continuous curve.

The horizontal coordinate is the polar angle θ measured from the cone axis. For this sharp-cone similarity model, properties depend on θ and do not depend on distance from the apex along a ray. At an axial station x from the apex, radial position is r = x tan θ, and the transverse shock-layer thickness is x(tan β − tan θc). A dimensional thickness therefore requires an axial station; it is not a single constant separation.

What the pictures mean

Normal/oblique shocks use uniform analytical regions. Expansion fans use the Prandtl–Meyer solution. Internal nozzle color follows section-averaged quasi-1D pressure. External-wave color represents a local planar upper-lip simple wave: a continuous Prandtl–Meyer fan or uniform states on each side of an oblique shock. Angles use equal geometric scale; the local view is cropped and does not represent the entire jet. Nozzle + external waves combines the complete schematic planar nozzle with mirrored first lip waves. Nozzle half-height follows area for constant span. The external construction ends at the first centerline interaction; hatching marks the unresolved region. For a sonic exit, combined-view fans are schematic, and their simple-wave details are available in the local view. Cone color follows the angular Taylor–Maccoll solution. Rayleigh/Fanno duct colors are an illustration of the two endpoint states, not a spatial solution. Shock-interaction positions follow the selected educational or custom geometry and the calculated wave angles; the view ends before downstream wall reflections. The model label below every scene states its interpretation.

Animated tracers are illustrative; they do not establish unsteady particle trajectories. The shock-tube animation uses analytical wave positions, with a continuous expansion fan, and applies before any end-wall reflection.

Control-volume experiment

Chapter 2 checks proposed inlet and outlet states. The outlet-speed mode enforces mass conservation only. Signed contributions sum to each residual; the relative residual is divided by the sum of the absolute contributions. Heat into the fluid, shaft work out and axial non-port force on the fluid in the flow direction are positive. An entropy-decrease check is provided for adiabatic, mass-balanced cases. Integral balance closure does not resolve the interior or establish the existence of a compatible flow. Endpoint colors never interpolate an interior field.

Mach input ranges

Numerical entry supports M up to 50 in the isentropic, normal-shock, control-volume, Rayleigh and Fanno laboratories; up to 20 in oblique waves, Prandtl–Meyer expansion, shock interaction, linearized airfoil and prescribed-inlet nozzle flow; and up to 10 in the conical-flow ODE laboratory. Slider upper limits match the numerical-entry limits, and typing a value updates the slider position. These are tested interface ranges, not guarantees of real-gas validity. Heat capacities remain fixed. Attached-shock, expansion, choking and prescribed-boundary compatibility constraints are still checked.

Important domain boundaries

  • Rayleigh: heat above qmax gives no steady outlet for the prescribed inlet. The inlet must adjust in a real system. No fabricated sonic outlet is shown for the excessive heat.
  • Fanno: fᴅL/D uses the Darcy convention, equal to 4fꜰL/D. Exceeding the sonic friction length gives no steady outlet with the fixed inlet.
  • Oblique shocks: attached weak and strong branches are separated. Beyond θmax, the app reports detachment without inventing detached geometry.
  • Shock interaction: opposing and same-direction interactions match downstream pressure and direction using weak attached shocks and centered expansion fans. Detached shocks and Mach reflections are outside this release.
  • Nozzle: the minimum area depends on geometry. A converging nozzle becomes sonic at its exit. A diverging passage requires an inlet Mach number; a prescribed back pressure can locate an internal normal shock, but a pressure above the shock-at-inlet limit cannot maintain that inlet. In External waves, the uniform axial exit feeds a local planar upper-lip pressure adjustment. Only a centered expansion or weak attached oblique shock is selected. Strong/detached patterns, opposite-lip interaction, reflections, shock cells, Mach disks, axisymmetric plume structure, mixing and separation are not solved. The ambient is a constant-pressure boundary. A sonic expansion is a local limiting solution.
  • Linearized airfoil: small slopes, small incidence and supersonic freestream are required. Near Mach 1, the approximation is strained.
  • Cone: axisymmetric flow at zero incidence. Adaptive RK4 step doubling, a surface-tangency event and weak-branch shooting are used. Strong and detached solutions are not included.

Pressure-sweep playback

In the nozzle laboratory, Play sweep changes only the back-to-inlet stagnation pressure ratio while geometry and inlet gas conditions remain fixed. The complete-nozzle view is selected for playback. Pause or scrub to inspect a state, reverse direction, change demo speed, or jump to exact regime thresholds. Playback is a sequence of steady solutions; demo time is not physical time and no transient wave speed is calculated. Unresolved external patterns remain explicitly marked. A fixed supersonic diverging inlet stops at its shock-at-inlet pressure limit; a fixed subsonic inlet cannot be swept independently. Playback pauses when the page is hidden.

External-wave relations

The local model uses the ideal-gas relations in NACA Report 1135: isentropic pressure matching and the Prandtl–Meyer function for expansion, and normal/tangential jump relations for an attached oblique shock. Pressure matching alone does not select the global jet pattern beyond the weak attached branch.

Wave Interaction Calculator · Chapter 6

Opposing shocks models the two-wall interaction in Figure 6–13. Same-direction shocks models two consecutive upward lower-wall turns in Figure 6–16. In same-direction mode the second angle is an additional turn, not the absolute final wall angle. In opposing mode each angle is a positive turn magnitude at its own wall. Axial corner separation is the upper corner x minus the lower corner x, with the lower corner at x = 0; for same-direction corners it is the axial distance between the two lower-wall corners. The signed slip-line angle is positive upward and is solved from equal downstream pressure and flow direction.

Educational mode selects normalized dimensions automatically. Custom geometry specifies the upper-wall height above the first corner and the axial corner separation in metres. Both use actual wave angles and equal horizontal and vertical scales. Uniform scaling changes coordinates without changing flow states. Incompatible geometries are rejected. The display ends before the earliest downstream wave or slip-line wall encounter; later wall reflections are not solved.

Uniform regions and sampled Prandtl–Meyer fans use analytical relations. Weak attached shocks are used; detached shocks, Mach reflections, viscosity and mixing are outside the model. Results include states 1–5 and one-sided values for every finite shock. This is not a CFD field.

Rayleigh and Fanno longitudinal contours

Both duct labs display blue-to-red rainbow, section-uniform one-dimensional contours of static pressure, Mach number, static temperature, density or flow speed. Colour varies along x/L; transverse gradients and boundary layers are not computed. Each colour scale uses the accepted interior states, not just the inlet and outlet extrema.

Rayleigh profiles assume uniform specific heat input per unit heated length: q(x) = qtotal x/L for a simple duct. Equal heat is added or removed per metre; static temperature need not vary linearly and is not uniform. This assumption is stated below the diagram and longitudinal charts and retained in image exports. Original property-versus-Mach relations remain available and do not depend on an axial heat distribution.

Fanno profiles assume constant friction factor and hydraulic diameter, so the cumulative friction parameter is proportional to x/L. Known shocks retain a discontinuous colour jump. The heat–shock–nozzle sequence retains its schematic shock/nozzle coordinate and uniform heating only in the heated duct segment; a contour does not determine an otherwise unspecified physical shock position. No interior field is shown beyond the model's choking limit.

Static and stagnation input choices

Pressure and temperature have independent static/stagnation selectors wherever conversion is direct at a known Mach number. This applies to freestream and inlet conditions, initially stationary shock-tube chambers, nozzle inlet conditions, supplied reference states, and specified sonic exits. Conversion uses the local station Mach and the selected gas properties. Static and stagnation quantities in an initially stationary reservoir or shock-tube chamber coincide.

Nozzle static inputs refer to the actual nozzle inlet section; its Mach follows the existing geometry and pressure-ratio solution. The back-pressure and ambient ratios retain their explicitly stated static-pressure numerators and inlet-stagnation-pressure denominators. These ratio controls are unchanged.

The original solution structures are retained. A static inlet temperature is available in the Fanno outlet-Mach/friction inverse because its inlet Mach is determined independently. The Rayleigh heat/outlet-Mach inverse retains inlet stagnation temperature; changing it to static temperature would introduce a different inverse problem. Outlet-pressure inputs with unknown outlet Mach retain static pressure. Where outlet Mach is specified, or a sonic exit is imposed, the static/stagnation pressure conversion is direct.

Shock–Expansion · direct relations and downstream wave tracking

In Supersonic Wave Lab, select Custom wall, Boundary layout = Channel and Channel solution = Shock–Expansion. Three ready-to-edit examples are provided: Shock reflections (M = 3, lower wall +5°, flat upper wall, 1 m inlet gap), Shock–expansion (both wall directions +2°), and Two expansion fans (lower −3°, upper +3°). Segment lengths remain actual lengths; the shared domain ends at the shorter axial wall extent. There is one Shock–Expansion option for both the book interactions and their downstream continuation. Method of characteristics remains the separate shock-free isentropic method. Saved selections from either former shock/expansion option automatically migrate to Shock–Expansion.

Shock–Expansion follows an ordered list of fronts downstream. It detects adjacent-front intersections, wall encounters and wall corners, solves each local event, and continues with the resulting fronts. Finite compressions use weak oblique-shock relations. Expansions use the Prandtl–Meyer relation with constant stagnation pressure. An interior interaction matches pressure and direction between the outer incoming streams, generating two outgoing wave families and, when required, a slip line. The stagnation pressure and entropy on each side are retained separately; the solver does not impose one global isentropic stagnation pressure after shocks. This also supports regular shock/contact refraction and later wall reflections.

Each selected event states how it was obtained: Direct shock–expansion relations for events whose states and locations do not depend on discretized expansion fronts; Approximate fan-front interaction or dependent state after a finite expansion front participates. This dependency is carried into subsequent stream states. The first interactions of Examples 6–4 and 6–5 remain direct, even if a later reflected-fan event is approximate. A field containing expansion fronts is labelled Mixed solution; this does not turn a direct book match into an approximate fan interaction.

Expansion interactions are approximate. A fan is represented by several finite expansion fronts, each no wider than Expansion-front width (default 0.5°). Its propagation direction is the mean characteristic direction of its two states. A finite expansion front is an approximation to a continuous fan, so local Riemann matching does not make the entire field exact. There is no finite-volume mesh or remote CFD service. This is an event-driven wave-front method, not the full improved-front algorithm of Witteveen, Koren and Bakker (2007). It does not claim an error bound or convergence theorem for arbitrary strong interactions.

Check front refinement = On repeats the calculation at half the front width (down to 0.125° internally). At the chosen profile station, 201 equally spaced transverse samples give mean absolute changes in pressure divided by inlet pressure and Mach divided by inlet Mach. A refinement comparison is shown only if both runs reach the full shared domain. This is sensitivity to front width, not a bound on total error; moving discontinuities can dominate pointwise differences. The maximum cross-section mass-flow discrepancy is also reported. The calculation stops before accepting a section whose discrepancy exceeds 0.5%; a small mass discrepancy alone does not certify accuracy.

Inspect event selects a wall turn, reflection or interior interaction. Its marker is shown in the diagram; the right column gives the selected direction or slip angle and downstream pressure. The table below lists its incoming and outgoing states, and shock reports give one-sided states for each outgoing finite shock of that event. For an interior event with equal pressure/direction but differing thermodynamic or velocity states, the reported common direction is the slip-line angle. There can be several slip lines with different directions; there is no single global slip angle for the whole channel. Profile station x/L controls cross-channel property plots. Moving the pointer over a solved contour reports the piecewise-constant local Mach, pressure, temperature, density and flow angle. JSON and CSV include every accepted event and its wave states, not just the selected event.

Only attached weak shocks and states that remain supersonic and downstream-hyperbolic in x are followed. The calculation stops at a sonic/subsonic state, upstream-directed characteristic, no regular weak-wave pressure match, shock detachment, excessive mass discrepancy, or event/front/display budget. The unsolved downstream area is blanked and the reason is reported; an interrupted calculation is not a complete solution. Maximum budgets are 1,600 events, 300 simultaneous fronts and 80,000 display cells. Long or densely interacting fans may reach a budget even while a physical solution exists. Shorten the geometry or use coarser fronts, then assess refinement over the shorter domain. Strong/Mach reflections, detached shocks, viscosity, separation, mixing and subsonic regions are excluded.

Verification includes an independent SciPy calculation of a +5° incident shock and three wall reflections (states and coordinates); the original exact first interactions in Examples 6–4 and 6–5; exact symmetric-fan characteristic-invariant centre and wall states; one-sided shock mass, normal momentum, tangential velocity and stagnation-temperature balances; mirror symmetry; and half-width sensitivity checks for both fan-interaction examples. The book 6–4 geometry now continues past the first reflected-wave wall encounter, but subsequently stops when the regular attached-wave model has no admissible interval. That later field is deliberately not invented.

Method of characteristics · smooth fields and fitted shocks

Custom wall + Channel retains two separate methods: Shock–Expansion and Method of characteristics. The example buttons retain the selected method, except Load expansion example, which explicitly selects MOC. Use the same wall geometry and inlet conditions to compare the two methods.

For straight/outward-turning channels, MOC transports the isentropic planar characteristic invariants. When a compression corner is present, MOC switches to a rotational formulation. Along C+ and C− it integrates dθ ± [√(M²−1)/(γM²)] d(ln p) = 0. Stagnation pressure is transported along streamlines; stagnation temperature remains constant. A shock has separate upstream/downstream meshes. Its tangent and downstream state are solved using a downstream characteristic compatibility condition and the normal/tangential shock jump relations. This is an independent numerical characteristic solution, not a relabelled finite-fan shock–expansion calculation.

The fitted topology supports one active attached shock, successive regular wall reflections and interaction with smooth expansion fans, including curved shocks and nonuniform stagnation pressure. Shock reflections and Shock + expansion can be solved with either method. Two incident shocks, a new compression shock while another is active, shock/shock/contact interaction topology, detached shocks, Mach reflections and sonic/subsonic continuation are not supported by fitted MOC. A new unresolved interior shock formed by smooth compression is not inserted automatically. The solver stops for an excessive compression gradient, mass imbalance or numerical budget instead of fabricating a downstream field. Use Shock–Expansion for its wider event-tracking topology.

Characteristic resolution is 31–201 points per smooth region for fitted MOC. Increase it and compare results; interpolation produces numerical diffusion and a small mass-flux discrepancy. Fitted MOC retains total enthalpy and the physical entropy increase across every shock. The summary reports mass-flux and shock-jump residuals. The fitted-point inspector reports both sides along each shock; JSON and CSV include every fitted station. Profile station x/L is beside the chart. Contours use local pressure, temperature, density and speed, with separate one-sided values at shocks. Model limits retain only the accepted upstream field.

The implementation and limits are documented in the repository validation record. Background: NACA Report 841 and NASA CR-3121, Appendix A. These references motivate the equations and fitting approach; this application is a narrower numerical implementation, not a reproduction of the complete SEAGULL code.

Supersonic Airfoil Lab · Chapter 9

Geometry entry defaults to segment angles and lengths. Angles are signed directions relative to the chord (positive upward), not successive corner turns. Lengths are actual straight segment lengths divided by chord, Lᵢ/c, not their x projections. Enter the initial segment(s); the last segment on each side is calculated automatically to close at the fixed trailing edge (x/c,y/c) = (1,0). Its angle and length appear in the Auto row. The diamond mirrors the upper surface; the asymmetric double wedge allows one entered segment per side plus closure. Custom panels support up to 19 entered segments per side plus closure, including add/remove controls. The engine converts a segment using Δx/c = (L/c) cos θ and Δy/c = (L/c) sin θ. Entered segments must stay before x/c = 1, use |θ| < 85°, and stay within |y/c| ≤ 0.3. The completed surfaces must not cross. Custom geometry can switch to the advanced Coordinates mode without changing the shape. Previous saved coordinate or dimension inputs are migrated to equivalent segments.

A common geometry supports a flat plate, a symmetric diamond with adjustable peak location, an asymmetric double wedge with a specified peak camber, or custom upper and lower piecewise-straight surfaces. Coordinates use chord c = 1. For custom panels, enter 2–21 x/c,y/c pairs per side, separated by semicolons or newlines; each surface must begin at 0,0 and end at 1,0. Coordinates must increase in x, remain within |y/c| ≤ 0.3, and surfaces cannot cross. The body-fixed x axis runs from leading edge to trailing edge. Positive α inclines the freestream upward relative to the chord; the upper surface expands and the lower surface compresses on a flat plate.

Shock–Expansion marches from the leading edge along each surface independently using weak attached oblique shocks or Prandtl–Meyer expansions at the actual change in flow angle. Each finite shock reports before/after properties. Attached shocks and supersonic surface states are required; unsupported solutions are flagged and omitted. These are uniform panel states. The actual airfoil edges are drawn in black while a solution is pending, then each edge is colored by its accepted panel Cp using a saturated rainbow scale. The interior remains neutral; no colored ribbon or filled exterior field is drawn. Thin solid black lines represent shocks, and blue multi-ray fans represent expansions. Finite local wave rays are schematic, and wave intersections, subsequent downstream wave interactions, finite wake thickness, detached shocks, viscosity, boundary layers and finite-span effects are not solved. A curved or blunt airfoil cannot be represented as a validated general solution merely by adding vertices.

Linearized theory uses Cp,U = 2(y′U − α)/√(M∞² − 1) and Cp,L = 2(α − y′L)/√(M∞² − 1), with α in radians. Surface perturbation potential is ψ = φ/(U∞c) = −½∫Cp dξ, where ξ = x/c; the perturbation velocity convention is u′ = ∂φ/∂x. Integration constants set ψ = 0 at the leading edge on each side, and ψ is continuous at each panel corner. This is a first-order surface approximation, evaluated on the chord reference line; it is not an exact potential field through finite shocks. Total surface potential is Φ/(U∞c) ≈ ξ + ψ to first order. Upper and lower potentials need not match at the trailing edge for lifting flow; a wake cut can carry the potential jump.

Panel pressure forces are integrated over the closed geometry. Nonlinear forces are resolved exactly along and normal to the incident velocity. The linearized coefficients retain first-order lift and moment and second-order wave drag; they are not simply an exact rotation of approximate pressures. The quarter-chord moment coefficient is positive nose-up, with reference x/c = 0.25. All coefficients use freestream q∞ = ½γp∞M∞² and unit chord. Drag is inviscid pressure/wave drag only. Compare mode uses the same shape and inlet for both models, shows Cp curves together, and reports signed differences SE − linear. Below the illustration, the Aerodynamic coefficients table compares lift, wave drag, quarter-chord moment, lift-to-drag ratio and the pressure coefficient Cp for each surface region. Cp is local to each region, whereas lift, drag and moment are integrated airfoil coefficients. A separate Flow properties by region table reports local surface states. Geometry intervals, panel angles and lengths are omitted from these tables; angle/length inputs and the automatic closing segment remain in the geometry editor. Coefficients are not repeated in the right-hand result list. Colored surface strips show Cp for the shock–expansion model when available, otherwise the selected linearized model; they do not imply a resolved exterior field.

Conical-shock attachment limits

Two reciprocal limits are reported for the selected gas: the maximum cone half-angle θc,max at the current freestream Mach number, and the minimum Mach number Mcrit admitting an attached conical shock at the current cone half-angle. The angle limit is the maximum of the Taylor–Maccoll cone-angle curve as shock angle varies, where the weak and strong branches meet. Mcrit is found by inverting that limit. Above θc,max, or below Mcrit, the shock must be detached within this ideal sharp-cone model. The limiting state itself is marginal attachment. The cone Mach input extends down to 1.0001, allowing the near-sonic critical Mach of slender cones to be inspected. These are shock attachment limits, not the sonic surface boundary and not viscous boundary-layer separation. Detached conditions keep the threshold outputs visible but do not show an invented flow field. The search uses the chosen γ and caches repeated results; changing only R does not affect the limits. A missing Mcrit means no root was resolved within the documented numerical search up to M = 128.

Airfoil trailing-edge matching

With Shock–Expansion or Compare both selected, the last upper and lower panel states feed a local two-stream Riemann match at the sharp trailing edge. The shared flow angle φ is solved from equal downstream pressure. Each side uses a weak attached oblique shock, a centered Prandtl–Meyer expansion, or no wave. The pressure and direction match; speed, density, Mach number and stagnation pressure can differ across the slip line. Identical matched streams are labelled Common streamline instead. TE-U− and TE-L− are the incident upper and lower streams; TE-U+ and TE-L+ are the matched downstream streams. This is a local inviscid construction, using the independently marched surface states. Unsupported trailing-edge matching is reported separately and does not remove valid surface forces. Important domain boundaries

  • Rayleigh: heat above qmax gives no steady outlet for the prescribed inlet. The inlet must adjust in a real system. No fabricated sonic outlet is shown for the excessive heat.
  • Fanno: fᴅL/D uses the Darcy convention, equal to 4fꜰL/D. Exceeding the sonic friction length gives no steady outlet with the fixed inlet.
  • Oblique shocks: attached weak and strong branches are separated. Beyond θmax, the app reports detachment without inventing detached geometry.
  • Shock interaction: opposing and same-direction interactions match downstream pressure and direction using weak attached shocks and centered expansion fans. Detached shocks and Mach reflections are outside this release.
  • Nozzle: the minimum area depends on geometry. A converging nozzle becomes sonic at its exit. A diverging passage requires an inlet Mach number; a prescribed back pressure can locate an internal normal shock, but a pressure above the shock-at-inlet limit cannot maintain that inlet. In External waves, the uniform axial exit feeds a local planar upper-lip pressure adjustment. Only a centered expansion or weak attached oblique shock is selected. Strong/detached patterns, opposite-lip interaction, reflections, shock cells, Mach disks, axisymmetric plume structure, mixing and separation are not solved. The ambient is a constant-pressure boundary. A sonic expansion is a local limiting solution.
  • Linearized airfoil: small slopes, small incidence and supersonic freestream are required. Near Mach 1, the approximation is strained.
  • Cone: axisymmetric flow at zero incidence. Adaptive RK4 step doubling, a surface-tangency event and weak-branch shooting are used. Strong and detached solutions are not included.

External-wave relations

The local model uses the ideal-gas relations in NACA Report 1135: isentropic pressure matching and the Prandtl–Meyer function for expansion, and normal/tangential jump relations for an attached oblique shock. Pressure matching alone does not select the global jet pattern beyond the weak attached branch.

Wave Interaction · Chapter 6

Opposing shocks models the two-wall interaction in Figure 6–13. Same-direction shocks models two consecutive upward lower-wall turns in Figure 6–16. In same-direction mode the second angle is an additional turn, not the absolute final wall angle. In opposing mode each angle is a positive turn magnitude at its own wall. Axial corner separation is the upper corner x minus the lower corner x, with the lower corner at x = 0; for same-direction corners it is the axial distance between the two lower-wall corners. The signed slip-line angle is positive upward and is solved from equal downstream pressure and flow direction.

The geometry-mode selector has been removed: one editable dimensional layout is used. Set the upper-wall height/clearance and axial corner separation in metres. Actual wave angles and equal horizontal/vertical scales are retained; uniform scaling changes coordinates without changing matched states. This calculator assumes that both incident shocks meet directly before a wall encounter. If an incident shock reaches a wall first, an on-screen message explains that its reflected wave changes the event sequence and offers Open Custom wall / Channel. This opens the general channel editor with Shock–Expansion selected; define the channel walls there to follow reflections. The shortcut opens that editor rather than claiming to transfer the same two-corner geometry automatically. Incident rays that do not meet downstream of both corners receive a separate explanation. The direct calculator still ends before the earliest outgoing-wave or slip-line wall encounter.

Uniform regions and sampled Prandtl–Meyer fans use analytical relations. Weak attached shocks are used; detached shocks, Mach reflections, viscosity and mixing are outside the model. Results include states 1–5 and one-sided values for every finite shock. This is not a CFD field.

Rayleigh flow: scope and known data

The single-duct model is steady, one-dimensional, constant-area and frictionless, with a calorically perfect gas. Heat input q is positive for heating and negative for cooling. Heating approaches Mach 1 from either branch. Shock-free Rayleigh flow does not cross Mach 1; cooling moves away from it. The model rejects heat beyond the fixed-inlet sonic limit, nonpositive temperatures and cooling beyond the finite supersonic branch. A sonic inlet under cooling needs a branch definition; use a slightly subsonic or supersonic inlet in the forward interface.

Single-duct modes accept heat with known inlet Mach, heat with known outlet Mach, a specified outlet Mach or static pressure, or a sonic outlet. The outlet-Mach heat mode infers the inlet on the same branch. Static and stagnation inlet pressure are converted using the solved inlet Mach. Heat slider bounds follow the active known data, including temperature and flow-speed inputs.

Reservoir-fed modes solve a subsonic duct inlet using supply stagnation conditions and either heat with a sonic outlet, heat with back pressure, heat with outlet Mach, or outlet Mach with outlet pressure. A back pressure below the heating-choked threshold does not become the duct exit pressure: the exit remains sonic and the external pressure adjustment is not resolved. A sonic outlet requires nonnegative heating in this subsonic-entry model. Unsupported or incompatible data produce an error, not a continuation across the sonic point.

When supply static pressure and reference Mach are specified, the upstream reference is moving. Its stagnation pressure is computed from those inputs; supply temperature is still a stagnation temperature. An ideal isentropic connecting streamtube preserves p₀ and T₀. The reported Ain/Aref = (ρu)ref/(ρu)in is the area ratio required by continuity. Equal reference and inlet areas are not imposed. A transonic entry nozzle is outside this model. Examples R1–R4 use this moving-reference interpretation. With stagnation-only supply data, a large stationary reservoir is shown instead.

The separate “Upstream reference + sonic outlet” case uses an initial operating state before the inlet adjusts to thermal choking. The reference and adjusted inlet can have different mass fluxes, even for an unchanged duct area; they are not two simultaneous equal-area stations. Its table and diagram identify the initial reference explicitly.

Heat, normal shock and converging nozzle

This supported sequence contains a supersonic Rayleigh segment, a normal shock and an isentropic converging nozzle with sonic outlet. Given the inlet state and sonic nozzle exit pressure, the solver determines the heat transfer and the states on both sides of the shock, together with the required exit/duct area ratio. A supersonic inlet is required. Detached shocks, viscous losses and other shock/nozzle arrangements are not solved.

The shock coordinate, heat distribution and nozzle shape in the illustration are schematic. The inputs determine station states, not the shock’s physical axial position. Plot distance x/L is a normalized schematic coordinate; uniform heat per normalized duct length is assumed. A physical shock location requires additional geometry and heat-distribution information.

Fanno nozzle and duct system

Select Nozzle + Fanno duct for a single reservoir → converging-diverging nozzle → constant-area duct geometry. Use the Examples dropdown above Computed state in the right column to load Examples 1–6 with their book data. Examples 3–5 share this geometry, with separate operating conditions and unknowns. Example 5 provides the three shock positions through Operating condition. The reservoir is at rest, so its static and stagnation pressures and temperatures coincide. The specified nozzle inlet/throat and exit/throat area ratios define a smooth quadratic area profile; nozzle and duct lengths locate stations on the complete x-coordinate. The nozzle is isentropic except at a normal shock. The adiabatic duct uses constant hydraulic diameter and friction factor, with Darcy parameter fᴅL/D = 4fꜰL/D.

Operating condition selects a matched supersonic no-shock state, the sonic-throat limit, a shock at the nozzle exit, a shock at the duct exit, or Sonic at duct exit. The matching back pressure is calculated. At the throat limit M = 1 and shock strength is zero: no finite jump is drawn. Shock location (vary back pressure) instead finds the steady shock position throughout the divergent nozzle and duct. The pressure slider, playback and exact landmark jumps use the same solver. For the usual shock branch, the demonstration stops at the duct-exit shock (or friction-choked exit bound) and the sonic-throat limit, excluding repeated fields that differ only by unresolved external pressure adjustment. The fully subsonic branch is omitted from this shock demonstration. For a duct too long to support any sonic-throat/shock branch, only the resolved subsonic interval from sonic duct exit to no flow remains available. A collapsed interval disables playback. Numeric pressure entry is bounded to the same interval; the separate fixed operating conditions remain available. Higher pressure moves the shock upstream, eventually reaching the throat limit and an unchoked, entirely subsonic branch. At equal back and reservoir pressure there is no flow.

Shock location (vary back pressure) determines shock location / flow regime from prescribed back pressure, so no separate Solve for control is shown for this condition. Solve for remains available for the other operating conditions. Each of the matched no-shock and three fixed shock conditions offers Back pressure, Reservoir pressure, or Friction parameter: all other necessary quantities are inputs, and pressure/friction bounds are checked on that same physical branch. Sonic at duct exit offers Back pressure or Reservoir pressure while the limiting friction parameter follows directly from the supersonic duct inlet Mach. The reservoir-pressure inverse for the sonic case is specific to that limiting no-shock branch; it is not a universal minimum for all flow regimes.

Nozzle length and inlet/throat area ratio are under the collapsed Nozzle geometry controls. For this ideal isentropic nozzle supplied by a reservoir at known stagnation conditions, nozzle length sets the drawing and physical axial shock coordinate; inlet/throat area sets the nozzle inlet state. Neither changes duct inlet Mach, pressure thresholds or limiting friction at fixed exit/throat area ratio. Axial shock position in metres requires the chosen area profile and length. Exit/throat area ratio remains a main input because it determines duct inlet Mach. Duct friction, unlike nozzle length, affects the flow state.

Not every shock station is possible for every friction length. Longer ducts may choke at a sonic duct exit with a stationary internal shock, or may prevent sonic conditions at the nozzle throat entirely. Unreachable presets are rejected; pressure sweeps retain the attainable limiting state and explicitly identify any unresolved external pressure adjustment. Supersonic exit static pressure need not equal back pressure. Station tables, longitudinal charts and contours preserve both sides of finite normal shocks. The single-duct forward/inverse model remains a separate option.

Validation and limitations

Automated checks cover heating and cooling over both Mach branches and several specific-heat ratios; independent mass, momentum and energy balances; forward/inverse consistency; static/stagnation pressure conversion; moving-reference entry continuity; back-pressure and zero-heat limits; and the heat–shock–nozzle component balances. Worked-example numerical checks are retained. These checks validate the implemented idealized models and do not establish accuracy for real reacting, viscous or variable-property flows.

Relations and choking behavior were checked against Georgia Tech AE3450 Rayleigh-flow notes. Entry continuity follows NASA Glenn mass-flow relations.
